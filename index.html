Approach

Translation is interpretation.
Localization is performance.

Every line in a game carries weight — a pause can build tension, a word choice can shift a character's entire persona, and a fragment left unfinished can say more than a full sentence. My work is built around protecting those details.

Tone over literalism

If a literal translation loses the emotional impact, I prioritise how the line is meant to feel. The player's experience matters more than a dictionary-perfect rendering.

Character voice integrity

Each character speaks differently for a reason. I build voice profiles — cadence, register, vocabulary boundaries — and hold every line accountable to them.

Transparent decision-making

Where a translation choice involves trade-offs, I document the reasoning and provide alternatives. No guesswork, no surprises — just clear options for the team.

Cultural fluency, not just linguistic

Having lived and worked across the UK, Italy, Spain, and Canada, I bring native cultural instinct to register, humour, formality norms, and the small details that make dialogue feel real.

Multilateral translation

I work fluidly between all four of my languages without needing to route through English — meaning I can localise directly from source to target, preserving nuance that's often lost in relay translation.

Character Voice & Tone Breakdown

Kane

Introspective · Confused · Emotional · Vulnerable

Kane's voice is built on fragmented thoughts, uncertainty, and fear. Short, broken cadence with simple words and ellipses that feel like thinking aloud. I deliberately avoided polished grammar — his panic and confusion need to live in the syntax itself.

Opening disorientation
ENWhere am I? I don't remember anything…
FROù suis-je ? Je ne me souviens de rien…
ITDove sono? Non ricordo niente…
Emotional escalation — profanity calibration
ENLook, fuck... I don't even know what I'm doing on this train.
FRÉcoute, putain... je sais même pas ce que je fous dans ce train.
ITSenti, cazzo... non so nemmeno che ci faccio su questo treno.
Localizer noteKane's lines preserve ellipses and fragmented phrasing across all targets. The vulnerability is carried as much by what's left unfinished as by the words themselves. For the profanity line, FR "putain" and IT "cazzo" are natural equivalents at a medium register — matching the emotional spill without pushing into gratuitous territory.

Phoebe

Calm · Enigmatic · Observant · Heavy subtext

Phoebe's meaning sits underneath her words. Clean, controlled lines with minimal emotional decoration. The real message is always implied, never stated — short sentences, direct delivery, and an unsettling sense that she knows more than she's saying.

Cryptic accusation
ENThe one you left behind before you got here. The one you don't want to face.
FRCelle que tu as laissée derrière toi avant d'arriver ici. Celle que tu ne veux pas affronter.
ITQuella che hai lasciato indietro prima di arrivare qui. Quella che non vuoi affrontare.
Controlled weight — saying everything with nothing
ENFear, guilt... or just habit.
FRLa peur, la culpabilité... ou juste l'habitude.
ITPaura, colpa... o semplicemente abitudine.
Localizer notePhoebe's power comes from restraint. The challenge is resisting the urge to add emphasis or explanation. Each translation preserves her clipped, knowing delivery — the articles in French ("la peur, la culpabilité") add a deliberate, almost clinical distance that works perfectly for her character.

Ray

Extroverted · Confident · Charismatic · Subtly evasive

Ray speaks with smooth, confident phrasing — friendly on the surface but always redirecting. Casual, slightly teasing language while avoiding crude slang. The charm stays intact while hinting that he's performing rather than being genuine.

Philosophical deflection
ENThe past is a passenger that never gets off. Secrets define you and you can't hide anything.
FRLe passé est un passager qui ne descend jamais. Les secrets te définissent, et tu ne peux rien cacher.
ITIl passato è un passeggero che non scende mai. I segreti ti definiscono e non puoi nascondere niente.
Condescending dismissal
ENAh, sure. Poor you. Always the victim.
FRAh, bien sûr. Pauvre petit. Toujours la victime.
ITAh, certo. Poverino. Sempre la vittima.
Localizer noteRay's dismissiveness needs to feel casual, not aggressive. FR "Pauvre petit" is more belittling than a literal "Pauvre toi" — it infantilises Kane, which serves Ray's power dynamic. IT "Poverino" carries the same condescending warmth. The sarcasm reads as character, not cruelty.

Dr. Lookman

Polite surface · Professional · Disturbing underneath

Lookman is the most complex voice to localise. Outwardly professional and measured, but with verb choices and timing that create unease. Controlled, clinical wording with unsettling pauses before reveals — polite sentences that make you uncomfortable without ever being overtly threatening.

The "safe" opening — polite menace
ENDon't worry, you're safe here... for now.
FRNe t'inquiète pas, tu es en sécurité ici... pour l'instant.
ITNon preoccuparti, qui sei al sicuro... per ora.
Surveillance disguised as care
ENLooks like you have to go... see you soon, I'll be watching you... Ray.
FROn dirait que tu dois y aller... à bientôt. Je te surveillerai... Ray.
ITSembra che tu debba andare... a presto. Ti terrò d'occhio... Ray.
Localizer noteThe register decision for Lookman was the most significant in the project. In real-world FR/IT therapy, "vous/Lei" is the norm. But the client chose informal "tu" — making Lookman's presence feel more intimate and invasive than professionally distant. FR "Je te surveillerai" and IT "Ti terrò d'occhio" both carry the polite-but-creepy surveillance subtext better than a neutral "I'll watch you."

4th Wall Posters

Meta · Taunting · Direct to player · Escalating intensity

The game features posters that break the 4th wall and address the player directly — taunting, mocking, and creating unease outside the narrative. Without knowing the target age rating, I delivered a safe default tone and provided harder alternatives for each, giving the client the ability to dial intensity up or down without a second localization pass.

Default tone — taunting
ENYou're still here. How obedient... Spoiler: you're not getting any.
FRT'es encore là. En manque de compliments ? Spoiler : t'en auras pas.
ITSei ancora qui. A caccia di complimenti? Spoiler: non ne avrai.
Harder alternative — cold and final
ENTime rewinds. I don't. Bye.
FRLe temps rembobine. Pas moi. Salut.
ITIl tempo riavvolge. Io no. Ciao.
Localizer noteThe posters required a different approach from dialogue — direct, punchy, no subtlety. I delivered each poster at a "safe" register and provided harder versions for the client to choose from. IT "riavvolge" keeps the VHS/tape-rewind meta feel; FR "rembobine" does the same. The brutally short "Pas moi. Salut." / "Io no. Ciao." lands harder in both languages than a longer construction would.

Key Localization Decisions

Register: "tu" vs formal address

+
FR and IT both have formal/informal distinctions that English doesn't. I defaulted to informal "tu" across all characters to keep dialogue immediate and intimate. The main exception considered was Dr. Lookman, where formal "vous/Lei" would add professional distance but informal "tu" creates a more invasive, unsettling closeness. Both alternatives were documented with reasoning — the client chose informal.

Briefcase: ambiguity vs specificity

+
Ray's briefcase is a recurring story object. The choice: generic terms ("mallette" / "valigetta" — ambiguous, fits mystery) vs specific ones ("ventiquattrore" in IT — explicitly an office briefcase, sharpening Ray's professional persona). I defaulted to ambiguous to serve the mystery tone. The client confirmed this preference.

"Loop" — game language vs story language

+
The game has time-loop mechanics. In Italian, keeping "loop" preserves a modern, meta, game-logic feel. In French, "boucle" works naturally and avoids an anglicism. I recommended keeping "loop" in IT and "boucle" in FR — respecting how each language handles borrowed terms differently. The client agreed.

4th-wall posters: tiered intensity

+
Without knowing the target age rating, I delivered each poster at a "safe" default and provided harder alternatives. This gave the client the ability to dial intensity up or down per poster without needing a second pass. The client opted for the harder versions.

Gender-neutral constructions

+
French and Italian are heavily gendered languages. Where the player character's gender isn't fixed, I restructured lines to avoid gender marking entirely. Dynamic UI strings were rewritten to be gender-safe regardless of what item name gets inserted. Where gendered forms were unavoidable, I flagged them with alternatives.

UI vs narrative tone separation

+
I treated UI strings differently from narrative text. Menu labels, tooltips, and system prompts use standard, conventional localization — players expect familiar terms. The atmospheric, unsettling tone was reserved for dialogue and narrative moments. This prevents player confusion while keeping the "weirdness" where it belongs.

Deliberate inconsistency as a narrative tool

+
In the Phoebe conversation, IT uses "arretri" (backtrack) in one line and switches to "fare marcia indietro" (reverse course) later. This isn't an error — the first feels accidental, the second feels deliberate, mirroring the shift in Phoebe's tone from observation to accusation. I documented this choice and offered a consistent alternative if the client preferred strict uniformity.
